# Does Every Question Mark Deserve a Betteridge?

## Summary
This essay explores the nuances of **Betteridge’s Law of Headlines**—the adage stating that any headline ending in a question mark can be answered with "no." While the law is often weaponized as a "sick burn" on the internet, the author investigates whether such dunking is always fair. Through a series of eight questions, the text weighs the utility of question-based titles against the internet's reward for overconfidence, ultimately questioning whether thoughtful, nuanced writing can survive without them.

---

## 1. Are Question Marks in Titles Always Bad?
To blog is to invite criticism and "dunking." I accept this, and sometimes wonder if my willingness to endure it is a comparative advantage. Still, one particular species of criticism puzzles me: the invocation of **Betteridge’s Law**, which states:

> *Any headline that ends in a question mark can be answered by the word **no**.*

This is frequently used as a sick burn: *You titled your article ‘Is this the world’s first gay caveman?’ because it’s not the world’s first gay caveman, but you wanted attention...* 

But I don't quite understand the rules. Are question marks in titles *always* bad? I suppose one could argue for putting the bottom line right up front, but "this title used a question mark" is rarely treated as a valid standalone insult.

## 2. Are Question Marks Only OK If the Essay Ends with a "Yes"?
Sometimes it seems like a title is only acceptable if the essay concludes with a full-throated "yes." But this rule is strange. If it were universally enforced, we could mentally convert *"Do blue-blocking glasses improve sleep?"* into *"Yes, blue-blocking glasses really do improve sleep!"* 

If so, what was the use of the question mark in the first place? If we allow questions that are *actual* questions, the answer must sometimes be something other than a definitive yes.

## 3. Is Betteridge’s Law Useful at All?
I think so. At a minimum, it labels a recognizable theory of journalism:
1. Traditionally, news articles put the bottom line up front.
2. News articles have incentives to make clear affirmative statements in headlines.
3. Therefore, if an article uses a question, it’s because the author couldn't justify a clear affirmative statement.

This theory isn’t 100% accurate, but it's accurate enough to deserve a name. However, Betteridge’s law is rarely invoked as a neutral observation; it is almost always deployed as a dunk.

## 4. Is Betteridge Dunking Ever Appropriate?
Yes. Consider some of the best (or worst) examples from John Rentoul’s book, *Questions to Which the Answer Is No!*:
* “Will Guam capsize?”
* “Is Osama Bin Laden in Chicago?”
* “Did Jesus foresee the US Constitution?”
* “Des smartphones bientôt équipés d’airbags?”

We can all agree something is fundamentally wrong with these titles—but what, exactly?

## 5. Is It Central That the Answer Is "No"?
Consider these made-up alternatives:
* “Is the Pope still Catholic?”
* “Do you need to sleep every day?”
* “Did Lincoln have personal qualms about slavery?”
* “Did the Rubicon even exist back when Caesar supposedly crossed it?”

These are **anti-Betteridges**. The answer to all of them is *yes*, but the titles are irritating in the exact same way: they manufacture a live debate where none exists.

## 6. What’s Really Going On Here?
Consider the title: *“Is aspartame bad for you?”* 

If you view aspartame's safety as settled science, and the article concludes it *is* safe, you'll find the title annoying. Conversely, if you think aspartame is dangerous and the article confirms it, you will *also* find the title annoying. 

The answer is immaterial. What’s truly irritating is when a title suggests a novel, interesting possibility that the article fails to substantiate as worthy of attention.

## 7. So What’s the Problem?
The modern internet rewards overconfidence. Bloggers aren't constrained by traditional newspaper norms; the best way to get attention is to write spicy, aggressive titles like, *"No, creatine does not make you smarter despite what dumb mouth-breathing hucksters tell you."*

You should say what you actually believe, but the internet corrupts us. We are gradually sculpted by the Reward Function into sensationalists. 

My favorite kind of essay is a lucid examination of all sides of an issue that refuses to force a definitive conclusion because the world is complicated. How is such an essay supposed to signal its nature if not by using a question?

## 8. What Should a Title Do?
One theory is that question titles are like [lists](https://dynomight.net/lists/): fundamentally sound formats that have been rendered suspicious by abuse. Under this view, we should push back against excessive Betteridge-ing and insist that question titles are fine when the question is genuinely open. 

A slightly more conciliatory version holds that if you use a question mark, it is your responsibility to prove it’s a real question, not a manufactured one. 

Part of me even wonders if question titles *should* be forbidden. I tried a *reductio ad absurdum* by giving this post a non-question title: *"I weakly think excessive Betteridge dunking disincentivizes fairly examining all sides of an issue."* At first, it sounded amusingly terrible. But is it?
