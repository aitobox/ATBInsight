# Directly Responsible Individuals (DRI)

### Summary
The concept of the "Directly Responsible Individual" (DRI)—a term popularized by Apple—defines the person ultimately accountable for the success or failure of a project. While this framework is essential for organizational clarity, it raises critical questions in the age of AI: can an LLM-powered agent ever be a DRI? This post argues that accountability is a uniquely human trait, and therefore, machines should never hold the ultimate responsibility for management decisions.

---

### The Origin of the DRI
The term **Directly Responsible Individual (DRI)** is best defined by the [GitLab Handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/people-group/directly-responsible-individuals/): it refers to the single person who is ultimately accountable for the success or failure of a specific project, initiative, or activity. Originating at Apple, this management philosophy ensures that there is no ambiguity regarding ownership and decision-making.

### AI Agents and the Accountability Gap
As we integrate LLM-powered agents into our workflows, it is tempting to assign them greater autonomy. However, I believe an AI agent should **never** be considered a DRI. 

Accountability requires the ability to take responsibility for one's actions—a capacity that machines fundamentally lack. When a project fails, there must be a human who can answer for the outcome, learn from the mistakes, and adjust the strategy. An agent, by contrast, operates without the moral or professional agency required for true accountability.

### Historical Context
This perspective aligns with a [legendary 1979 IBM training slide](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Feb/3/a-computer-can-never-be-held-accountable/), which succinctly states: 
> *"A computer can never be held accountable, therefore a computer must never make a management decision."*

While technology has evolved significantly since 1979, the core principle remains as relevant as ever: we must ensure that human oversight remains at the helm of all critical decision-making processes.
