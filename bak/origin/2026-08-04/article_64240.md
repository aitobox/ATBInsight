# Summary

In this edition of *Pluralistic* (July 31, 2026), Cory Doctorow explores the cultural and legal pitfalls of asking for permission for uses that are already covered by Creative Commons, fair use, *de minimis* exceptions, or simple common sense. Reflecting on the 25-year history of Creative Commons, Doctorow argues that permission-culture and corporate bullying have conditioned people into needless timidity. He advises readers on the realities of copyright boundaries, urging them to recognize the vast gray area of "harmless" technical infractions where involving authors and publishers only creates bureaucratic gridlock. The newsletter also features curated reading links, retro archives, upcoming event dates, and updates on his latest books.

From its inception, I’ve loved Creative Commons. I hung out with Lisa Rein, Matt Haughey, and Aaron Swartz while they coded the first version of the site, and my first novel, *Down and Out in the Magic Kingdom*, was the first professionally published text ever released under a CC license just weeks after the platform launched.

In those early days, CC licenses were primarily of interest to people steeped in copyright law. Many debates turned on esoteric questions about how CC would interact with copyright's "limitations and exceptions."

Copyright has *never* meant the absolute right to control all uses of a work. Every system includes limitations and exceptions for people making use of copyrighted works without permission—*even if the copyright holder objects*. The best-known example is American "fair use."

## The Nuances of Fair Use and Copyright Exceptions

Fair use is famously "fact-intensive"—meaning the legal answer is almost always "it depends." Courts evaluate fair use through a four-factor test:
1. **The purpose and character of the use** (e.g., transformative works, criticism, education).
2. **The nature of the copyrighted work** (factual vs. creative).
3. **The amount and substantiality** of the portion used.
4. **The effect of the use** upon the potential market for or value of the original.

However, running through these four factors as a simple checklist misunderstands the law. Fair use is ultimately subject to "the rule of reason"—meaning the law shouldn't result in obviously stupid restrictions. 

Beyond fair use, there is **"first sale,"** the doctrine ensuring that once you buy an item (like a used book or record), you own it, and copyright cannot interfere with your traditional property rights. Furthermore, copyright recognizes **"de minimis"** infractions—trifles so small that "the law does not concern itself with them" (such as a fleeting snapshot of a book page posted to social media).

These flexibilities are *your* rights as a member of the public. They apply without permission from rightsholders, and they apply *even if the rightsholder is furious*.

## The Trap of Permission Culture

Creative Commons successfully streamlined licensing for ambitious collaborations—like animating a Japanese short story using Italian 3D models and an Indonesian soundtrack. 

However, 25 years later, society has leaned heavily into a culture of timidity: *better safe than sorry*. Creators are constantly bombarded with permission requests for uses that are:
1. **Explicitly permitted** by existing CC licenses.
2. **Clearly covered** by fair use or *de minimis* exceptions (like reading a brief passage on YouTube or quoting a story in an exam).
3. **Technically copyright-infringing** (like a tiny bootleg run for an anarchist book fair or posting excerpts to a classroom intranet), but where asking the author forces them to say no because of contractual obligations to publishers.

When you email an author to ask permission for a trivial or technically unauthorized use, you often trap them. Bound by publishing contracts, they cannot grant authorization—forcing them to say no or loop in legal teams over trivial matters that no one would ever otherwise discover. 

As John Hodgman and I might disagree on, you don't always need to ask the cashier. Sometimes, the safest, most human approach is to recognize the vast universe of harmless technical infractions and simply *do it*.

Life is hard. Read books.
