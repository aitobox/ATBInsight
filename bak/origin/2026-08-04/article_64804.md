# Two Critical Updates: OpenAI’s Astra and the Future of Mathematics

### Summary
This update examines the recent hype surrounding OpenAI’s "Astra" model, suggesting it may be more marketing than a revolutionary breakthrough. By highlighting a rapid partial replication by an Anthropic researcher and referencing insights from mathematician Terence Tao, the analysis argues that while AI can solve specific, searchable math problems, it remains far from achieving genuine theoretical innovation.

---

## 1. Reassessing the "Astra" Breakthrough
My recent analysis of OpenAI’s [Astra](https://open.substack.com/pub/garymarcus/p/openais-amazing-but-vastly-oversold?r=8tdk6) emphasized that the company’s public communications often prioritize marketing over scientific transparency. 

Evidence for skepticism has only grown following insights from [Levent Alpöge](https://alpo.ge/), an accomplished mathematician at Anthropic. Alpöge was able to replicate half of OpenAI’s results within 24 hours using the older, publicly available *Fable* model. This leads to two critical conclusions:

*   **The "Search and Verify" Limitation:** OpenAI’s success may stem from identifying which open problems are amenable to specific search-and-verify techniques, rather than a fundamental leap in model intelligence. Because OpenAI failed to disclose the "denominator"—the problems they attempted and failed to solve—the true scope of Astra’s capability remains obscured.
*   **Incremental vs. Quantum Leap:** The fact that a previous model could achieve similar results suggests Astra is an incremental improvement rather than a paradigm shift. This is further supported by reports that OpenAI is still debating whether to brand the model as "GPT-6" or "GPT-5.7," a hesitation unlikely to exist if they believed they had achieved a true quantum leap.

## 2. Terence Tao on AI and Mathematics
A longtime reader directed me to a [compelling lecture by Terence Tao](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/age-of-ai-icm-2026.pdf), one of the world’s leading mathematicians. His perspective is essential for anyone tracking the intersection of AI and formal logic.

Tao introduces the concept of **"proof indigestion"**: the risk that AI will generate vast quantities of mathematically correct but ultimately useless information. He draws a vital distinction between two types of mathematical labor:
1.  **Solving Open Problems:** An area where AI is showing promise, albeit with specific, narrow constraints.
2.  **Building Theory:** A creative, structural process for which there is currently no evidence that AI is capable.

Tao is not anti-AI; rather, he advocates for a thoughtful integration of these tools into the mathematical community. His lecture serves as a sobering reminder that being good at generating proofs is not synonymous with contributing to the broader, deeper goals of mathematics.

### A Note on Brandolini’s Law
As we navigate this era of AI-generated content, it is worth keeping **[Brandolini’s Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandolini's_law)**—the "bullshit asymmetry principle"—in mind. As engineer Wouter Vreugdenhil aptly noted, the current landscape of AI hype often feels like a race to see who can produce the most noise, regardless of its underlying value.
