# Lawyer for Apple Mixed Up Two OpenAI Employees’ Names, Sent One Email to the Wrong Guy, Back in February

*[Source: Daring Fireball / NBC News](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2026/07/16/apple-lawyer-name-mixup)*

---

## 📌 Executive Summary

Newly reviewed emails reveal that OpenAI *did* initially respond to Apple’s February outreach regarding alleged trade secret theft—contrary to Apple’s lawsuit claims. However, communications stalled after an outside Apple attorney (Gabriel Gross of Weil, Gotshal & Manges) mistakenly confused the names and email addresses of two OpenAI employees with rhyming surnames (Wang and Chang), emailing the wrong person and apologizing the next day. 

While OpenAI's general counsel was reportedly unsettled enough by the blunder to request the lawyer's removal—a request Apple denied—commentary suggests that OpenAI's interpretation of the mix-up as a total resolution to the matter is unpersuasive and unlikely to hold up as a substantive defense.

---

## 🔍 The Incident: What the Emails Show

According to reporting by David Ingram for NBC News, Apple's recent lawsuit alleged that OpenAI "never responded" to its concerns regarding trade secret theft. However, email records tell a more nuanced story:

* **The Initial Outreach:** OpenAI responded in February to Apple’s initial letters detailing claims of trade secret malfeasance.
* **The Mix-Up:** Gabriel Gross, an outside attorney representing Apple, intended to email an OpenAI employee named **Wang** (a former Apple employee), who had just called Gross to offer cooperation. 
* **The Wrong Recipient:** Instead, Gross accidentally replied on an email chain to a different OpenAI employee named **Chang**, confusing their interactions.
* **The Apology:** By the following morning, Gross realized his error and sent a clarification to Chang:
  > *“After we had emailed Mr. Wang yesterday about retaining Apple information, he promptly called me and offered to cooperate with Apple in resolving any issues. I then intended to email him again, but accidentally replied to my email chain with you instead. I apologize for the confusion that likely caused.”*

---

## ⚖️ The Fallout and Skepticism

Following the mistaken email, OpenAI's general counsel was reportedly upset by the gaffe and asked Apple to remove Gross from the legal matter—a request Apple declined. 

According to OpenAI spokesperson Pusateri, Chang believed the issue had been entirely resolved based on the errant email and thus chose not to respond further. 

### Critical Analysis

The narrative that a simple administrative name-and-email mix-up completely derailed communications and led OpenAI to believe the entire trade secret dispute was magically "settled" strains credulity. The back-and-forth effectively played out as follows:

1. **Apple Lawyer:** *“Here’s a letter and three exhibits laying out our claims of trade secret malfeasance.”*
2. **Apple Lawyer (to the wrong person):** *“Thanks for the phone call.”*
3. **OpenAI Lawyer (who had not yet responded):** *“What the heck? I never called this guy.”*
4. **Apple Lawyer:** *“Sorry, that second email wasn’t for you; I made a mistake.”*

To suggest that OpenAI’s legal team logically deduced from this sequence that the broader, high-stakes accusations of trade secret theft were suddenly a non-issue makes little sense. If this constitutes the core of OpenAI's defense regarding the breakdown in communication, their legal position may be much weaker than anticipated.
