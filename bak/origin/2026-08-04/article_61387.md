# Art Doesn't Scale

### Summary
In an era where AI-generated content is increasingly positioned as a replacement for human creativity, this essay explores the fundamental disconnect between "producers" and "appreciators." Drawing on the experience of waiting for a stalled comic book series, the author argues that art is not merely a product to be consumed, but a record of human effort, sacrifice, and intent. By comparing AI output to art forgeries, the piece posits that the value of art lies in the "bother"—the human choice to create—and that efficiency is a poor substitute for the emotional resonance of a work that carries the weight of its creator's life.

***

### The Smell of Ink and Nerds
I love books of all sorts, including the trashy ones. Nothing beats the visceral experience of a comic book shop: the slippery covers, the acidic smell of paper, and the thrill of discovering a series like *The Black Monday Murders*. 

The premise of that series—that money and magic are linked and banks worship a god of greed—is genius. But the series has been stalled for years. When I expressed my frustration to a tech-optimist friend, he offered the classic "Boomer" solution: *Why don't you just use AI to make your own?* 

To him, this is democratization. To me, it feels like being told that if you’re hungry, you should just cook the family puppy. It isn't a tribute; it’s a forgery. Removing the creative context of a work renders it hollow.

### What Value Does a Forgery Have?
The philosopher Denis Dutton once wrote extensively on "Artistic Crimes." His work highlights a crucial truth: if a masterpiece is revealed to be a fake, our assessment of it changes instantly. If we claim that the "fake" is just as good because it looks the same, we are admitting that art has no aesthetic basis—that it is all just "slop" designed to trigger a reaction.

We value works like *Stardew Valley* or *Good Will Hunting* not just for the final product, but for the human story behind them. We delight in the knowledge that a person sacrificed years of their life to bring a vision to fruition. When I walk through a museum, I am humbled by the fact that I *cannot* do what those artists did. They possess a spine and a conviction that I lack. AI, by contrast, is a misrepresentation of achievement. It wants the credit for the work without the labor of the soul.

### The Question of Efficiency
The tech-optimist argument centers on bottlenecks. They see a stalled comic series and see a "capacity problem" that needs to be solved with automation. 

I am not a luddite; I enjoy the convenience of a fast-food burger as much as the next person. But the difference is that I get to *choose*. The current AI rollout is designed to remove that choice, replacing human-made goods with cheaper, automated alternatives while keeping the price—and the pretense—the same. 

Sometimes, the honest answer to scarcity is simply *going without*. 

The proponents of AI keep trying to show us "better" outputs, thinking we are arguing about quality. We aren't. We are arguing about what it means to receive a gift from another human being. Every comic in my pull list is a letter from a stranger who cared enough to spend years of their life making something for me to find. 

**A machine cannot bother. It can only produce.**

I would rather have eight issues of a masterpiece and the ache of never getting a ninth than a thousand AI-generated issues that continue the story forever. The ache is part of the art. The waiting is part of the art. Forgeries don't ache; they just fill space, like the generic prints in a hotel lobby, hung there simply because a wall needed to be covered.

All hail God Mammon. He always did prefer the cheaper option.
