# "Rights for robots" and the AI slavery fantasy

While the AI bubble is primarily a *material* phenomenon—driven by the calculation that bosses are easy marks for a sales pitch promising to replace workers with software—there is an inescapable ideological component to it: the desire for a world without people in it.

AI dangles the possibility of a world free from ego-shattering confrontations between bosses who believe they're in charge and workers who know how things actually work and insist that management's ideas are dangerous, illegal, or unworkable. A world without people might be lonely, but it sure is convenient. How maddening it must be to invest billions in warehouse automation, only to have to halt the machines so human "components" can stop to pee. 

With AI, the fact that you need to pee—or get paid—becomes *your* problem rather than your boss's. After colleagues are fired "because AI will do their jobs," surviving workers realize plenty of desperate people will gladly step in if they complain too much about their bladder or their paycheck. Better yet, the human element can be outsourced overseas, allowing bosses to set-and-forget requirements without ever considering actual labor conditions. This is the illusion of full automation, where the AI does the job "like magic."

The "magic"? A human being trapped in an algorithmic Omelas, tormented by an inhuman pace and pitiless punishments without appeal. Often, "AI" simply stands for "Absent Indians": low-waged call-center workers pretending to be automated bots.

## The Morality of the Machine

The biggest difference between a machine and a person is **moral consideration**. A person deserves and demands moral consideration for their wellbeing, feelings, and biological needs. A machine gets none of this: you can curse at it, kick it, and bark orders without a "please" or "thank you."

There is only one kind of person you get to treat like this: **a slave.** 

Slavery is labor without even the pretense of moral consideration. AI is the fantasy of a world without people... except for slaves. It envisions skilled workers replaced with pliable chatbots, and stressed-out cab drivers replaced by remote-overseen "robo-taxis" whose drivers' personal struggles can be permanently ignored.

## The Trap of "Robot Rights"

The "AI safety" movement acts as a key hype mechanism, shifting focus away from AI's poor economics and substandard output by framing it as an existential risk. The notion that training a word-guessing program on too many texts will accidentally spawn a paperclip-maximizing "superintelligence" is absurd—akin to believing faster-bred horses will eventually foal a locomotive. Yet, framing AI as an X-risk makes it look godlike and immensely valuable.

Once the superintelligence myth takes hold, it pulls other thought experiments in its wake—including "rights for robots." While extending rights to natural things (like animals or watersheds) benefits human thriving by teaching us to treat each other better, extending personhood to *artificial* constructs has historically been a catastrophe. Corporate personhood unleashed immortal colony organisms ("limited liability corporations") that treat humans as disposable gut flora. 

Extending personhood to AI follows the corporate model: as soon as you debate whether chatbots have rights, you elevate them to personhood. Because they are designed to receive zero moral consideration, they are codified as **slaves**. Ultimately, the "rights for robots" discourse validates the AI salesman's core pitch: that the tech world has successfully invented a brand-new class of uncomplaining, rightless slaves.
