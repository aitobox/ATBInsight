## Summary

Following the massive success of *SimCity 2000*, Maxis entered a turbulent era of corporate expansion, public trading, and creative diversification in the mid-1990s. Attempting to branch out from pure entertainment, the company experimented with corporate training software, esoteric government simulations like *SimHealth*, and kid-friendly titles (*SimTown*, *SimPark*, *SimSafari*). While games like *SimTower*, *SimIsle*, and *SimCopter* found devoted audiences and brought fresh mechanics to the "Sim" formula, heavy brand dilution, an ill-fated IPO, and development stumbles severely wounded the company. 

By 1997, Maxis was facing mounting financial losses, leading to an acquisition by Electronic Arts. Under new leadership and a return to the tried-and-true isometric formula, Maxis ultimately found its footing again with the runaway success of *SimCity 3000*, setting the stage for Will Wright's next monumental creation.

## From Bureaucracy to Skyscrapers: The Mid-90s Expansion

Maxis came into 1994 riding high on the wings of *SimCity 2000*, but their immediate follow-up was the startlingly esoteric ***SimHealth: The National Health Care Simulation***. Far from a playful hospital management game, *SimHealth* was a literal simulation of government bureaucracy, asking players to solve complex dilemmas regarding insurance coverage, costs, and medical research. 

Born out of venture capitalist pressure from Warburg Pincus Ventures to create enterprise training tools, *SimHealth* originated from a Markle Foundation request during President Bill Clinton's administration. Though it featured an "Assumptions" menu to tweak core rules, players quickly discovered that adopting an extreme libertarian ideology—eliminating federal healthcare and cutting government services—could effortlessly "break" the simulation. Despite selling roughly 50,000 copies, the experiment highlighted the limits of modeling complex real-world systems as simple video games, and Maxis quickly wound down its corporate-simulation division.

### The Rise of *SimTower*

Maxis found much greater commercial success later that year with ***SimTower: The Vertical Empire***, conceived and coded in Japan by Yoot Saito. After passionately cornering Will Wright at a MacWorld exhibition, Saito pitched a *SimCity* contained entirely inside a single skyscraper. Centered around the frantic logistics of elevator management—and packed with charming details like visiting food critics and flying Santas—*SimTower* became a surprise hit, giving Maxis its second consecutive lucrative holiday season.

## The IPO, Missteps, and Brand Dilution

Buoyed by *SimTower*’s success, co-founder Jeff Braun made what he would later call "a big mistake": taking Maxis public on May 25, 1995. Caught in the early wave of the dot-com boom and the soaring educational software market, Maxis ballooned in ambition, opening a glitzy office in Walnut Creek, California, and releasing a flood of new titles that diluted the "Sim" brand into a state of confusion.

- ***SimTown*:** A kid-friendly, simplified version of *SimCity* that suffered from perceived adult condescension and poor sales.
- ***SimIsle: Missions in the Rainforest*:** Developed with British studio Intelligent Games, this tropical archipelago simulator stands as a hidden gem of the catalog. It introduced individual "agents" with distinct skills, forcing players into agonizing tradeoffs between economic exploitation and ecological preservation.
- ***SimGolf* & *SimPark*:** Straightforward niche titles that cluttered the market and failed to stand out against competitors like the *Links* golf series.
- ***SimCity 2000: Network Edition*:** An online multiplayer variant of the classic city-builder that struggled to find an audience due to an awkward fit for multiplayer mechanics and an unupdated interface.

## The SimWorld Initiative and *SimCopter*

While stepping back from day-to-day development, Will Wright worked on the "SimWorld" project—an effort to make Maxis games interoperable so they could share data and save files (such as loading a *SimCity 2000* map into another title).

The first major manifestation of this was 1996's ***SimCopter***, Maxis's first 3D vehicular simulator. Players could import their custom *SimCity 2000* cities and pilot helicopters through them on rescue missions, firefighting, and police chases. While pleasantly forgiving and engaging, the game suffered from rushed development to hit the Christmas shopping window. 

*SimCopter* also courted bizarre controversy when a gay developer embedded unapproved sprites of shirtless men in Speedos kissing each other to protest the industry's "aggressive heterosexuality." Though quickly patched out by Maxis, the ensuing media firestorm added to the mounting narrative of a company spiraling out of control.

---

## Acquisition by Electronic Arts and *SimCity 3000*

By mid-1996, stock prices plummeted from $50 to around $10, losses mounted past $2 million, and a 3D reboot of the next *SimCity* floundered in technical difficulties. In August 1996, the board forced Jeff Braun out, replacing him with former Disney executive Sam Poole, whose primary mandate was finding a buyer.

In June 1997, **Electronic Arts stepped in**, acquiring Maxis in a $125 million stock swap. EA viewed Maxis essentially as a one-product studio centered entirely around the *SimCity* brand. 

EA parachuted in manager Luc Barthelet to restore discipline. Barthelet unceremoniously scrapped the troublesome 3D engine for the next *SimCity*, returning the team to the tried-and-true isometric perspective with strict focus and structure. 

The resulting ***SimCity 3000*** shipped in February 1999 to critical acclaim and massive commercial success, becoming the best-selling single computer game of 1999 and selling over 7 million copies across the franchise. Interestingly, Will Wright contributed practically nothing to the sequel, proving that Maxis could build a *SimCity* masterpiece without him. 

While the era of quirky experimental simulations had largely drawn to a close under EA's corporate roof, Wright was quietly working on an entirely different kind of simulation—one that was about to turn the entire gaming industry completely upside down.
