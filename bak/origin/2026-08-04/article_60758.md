# Summary

In this July 14, 2026 edition of *Pluralistic*, Cory Doctorow explores the precarious nature of political and institutional gerontocracies through the lens of actuarial reality. Tying the concept of the American "designated survivor" to the advanced age of modern political leadership—spanning the U.S. legislature, the judiciary, and the executive branch—Doctorow argues that clinging to power past one's prime without adequate succession planning invites institutional catastrophe. The essay is accompanied by Doctorow's signature daily link roundup, historical retrospectives ("Object permanence"), upcoming and recent public appearances, a comprehensive bibliography of his latest and upcoming books, and the standard colophon and licensing details.

# Gerontocracy's Failure Mode

![The Angel of Death, peering down from a break in a menacing sky full of clouds, looking upon the Capitol Dome, wreathed in spooky mist. To the Capitol's left is a spooky graveyard.](http://localhost/proxy/BPcAUhjKpem1SlBOCMPnMOmyIq-jEKwU8iJECqMIqqU=/aHR0cHM6Ly9pMC53cC5jb20vY3JhcGhvdW5kLmNvbS9pbWFnZXMvYWN0dWFyeS1nZXJvbnRvY3JhY3kuanBnP3c9ODQwJnNzbD0x)

The "designated survivor" is one of the weirder aspects of America's (very, very weird) political system. Each year, during the State of the Union address, when both houses of Congress and the President are all under one roof, a single political figure in the line of presidential succession is spirited away to a hidden bunker, just in case the legislative and administrative branches are wiped out in a single catastrophic strike ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Designated_survivor)).

Initiated during the 1950s, designated survivors are a paranoid relic of the Cold War, but they are also a product of an era when America was a more technocratic land. It’s a longtermist procedure that sharply contrasts with vibes-based political chaos, where daily announcements introduce new wars, tariffs, and existential threats. 

America's ruling class has always sought an equilibrium between its pure Id—hatred for labor, autocratic yearnings, and apocalyptic fantasies—and its patient, scheming Ego, the author of Federalist Society nominee lists and decades-long schemes to overturn *Roe v. Wade*. The problem arises when the impulsive elements escape containment, generating turbulence that pulls everyone into a chaotic vortex. How can you plan for *anything* when buffeted by endless stunts and distractions?

Nowhere is this failure to plan more vivid than in the advanced age distribution of the US legislature, presidential candidates, and judicial appointments across both major parties. The equilibrium of key institutions is brittle: legislative majorities are razor-thin, federal circuits and the Supreme Court hang in knife-edge balances, and presidential races repeatedly pit septuagenarians against octogenarians.

The question isn't whether older people can be effective; they certainly can. The problem is **actuarial**: older people are *far* more likely to die or suffer severe medical episodes. At 55, losing peers annually is a stark reality. Ignoring this baseline fact of life sets leaders and their projects up for disaster.

This principle applies everywhere. As a writer, it means planning for the smooth management of a literary estate. While my longtime friend John Scalzi is an exceptional choice for literary executor, he is older than I am. To hedge against actuarial reality, I recently added a second executor, Molly White, who is 20 years younger and brings relevant experience as the executor for her great-grandfather, E.B. White. That is what planning is all about: thinking through low-likelihood, high-salience scenarios and taking proportionate steps to avoid disaster.

This makes America's political gerontocracy so baffling. The nonagenarians, octogenarians, septuagenarians, and late sexagenarians in the US Senate know they could keel over at any moment—yet in a tightly divided Senate, a sudden vacancy spells absolute chaos for their political projects. We see a legislature where members disappear for months only to be discovered in care homes with advanced dementia ([Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/magazine/2025/03/14/kay-granger-dementia-dc-media-00210317)), or grandees clinging to power until death's door ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dianne_Feinstein#Personal_life)). Special elections triggered by sudden deaths have wildly unpredictable consequences.

While anyone can die suddenly—as I was tragically reminded 15 years ago when a dear friend passed away at just 44 ([Memex](https://memex.craphound.com/2012/06/28/eulogy-for-erik-possum-man-stewart/))—the statistical probability climbs sharply with age. Ignoring this can throw decades of long-term planning into disarray ([The American Prospect](https://prospect.org/2026/07/13/budget-consequences-of-lindsey-grahams-sudden-departure/)). 

Consider the Supreme Court: its slide into a handmaiden of totalitarianism accelerated when the dying Ruth Bader Ginsburg refused to step down under a Democratic president, substituting strategic caution for hubris ([New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/09/21/magazine/ginsburg-successor-obama.html)). Security practitioners know that overconfidence is the ultimate vulnerability; if you believe you are immune to hacks or scams, you are a danger to yourself and others ([Pluralistic](https://pluralistic.net/2024/02/05/cyber-dunning-kruger/#swiss-cheese-security)). Similarly, any politician in their 70s or 80s who assumes they are immune to strokes, cognitive lapses, or sudden health crises poses a direct threat to their party, their platform, and their nation. Actuarial tables are cold, hard realities—and pretending you can dictate the exact time and manner of your own passing is pure delusion.
