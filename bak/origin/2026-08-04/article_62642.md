# Pluralistic: California's Privacy Obstacle Course
**Date:** 23 July 2026

### Summary
In this edition of *Pluralistic*, Cory Doctorow critiques California’s new "DROP" (Delete Request and Opt-out Platform) initiative. While intended to help citizens reclaim their privacy from data brokers, the system is designed as a labyrinthine, redundant, and unnecessarily invasive bureaucratic hurdle. Doctorow argues that this "privacy theater" maintains the false premise that citizens want to be surveilled, rather than simply banning the predatory data broker industry outright.

---

## California's Privacy Obstacle Course
Data brokers are a cancer, fueling everything from phishing scams and identity theft to algorithmic wage discrimination. Despite the clear harm, federal and state governments have largely failed to ban these entities, opting instead for complex "compromises."

### The Failure of DROP
California’s new **DROP** platform is the latest attempt to manage this issue. To use it, citizens must navigate a series of redundant, high-friction authentication steps, including:
*   **Login.gov integration:** An already invasive process requiring multiple IDs and selfies.
*   **Redundant Verification:** Users are forced to photograph their driver's license or passport *after* having already used those same credentials to verify their identity via Login.gov.
*   **Manual Data Entry:** Users must manually input information—like their car’s VIN or mobile advertising identifiers—that the state already possesses or could easily retrieve.

Doctorow notes the irony: while it takes seconds for a data broker to start spying on you, it takes a Herculean effort to opt out. The system is built on the absurd fiction that surveillance is the default state and that citizens must be "defended" against the risk of someone *accidentally* opting them out of being tracked.

### The "Murder Offset" Analogy
Doctorow compares this to the "carbon credit" fiasco. Just as we don't create "murder offsets" to incentivize people not to kill, we shouldn't be creating "opt-out" platforms for surveillance. We should simply ban the practice. He concludes that while you *should* use the DROP tool to protect yourself, we must recognize it as a failure of policy—a "locked filing cabinet in a disused lavatory"—rather than a genuine solution.
