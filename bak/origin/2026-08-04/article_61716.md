# Make It Work vs. Make It Good

> **Summary:** A reflective look at the eternal tension between engineering and design. While "making it work" offers immediate novelty, low friction, and loud applause, "making it good" requires navigating nuance, subjective taste, and relentless attention to detail—proving that the hardest part of any project isn't getting it to function, but making it truly seamless.

---

There are two wolves inside of me, lol. 

Some days I want to be a “designer.” Other days I want to be a “developer.”

On the days I find myself wanting to feed the developer, it’s often because making something “work” seems easier (and more impressive) than making something “good.”

## The Allure of Functionality

Making something function often results in a reaction of: 

> *"Wow, that’s so cool! It didn’t work before and now it does! And I could’ve never made that, nice job!"*

And sometimes it’s like, good job, [you made a bear ride a unicycle](https://frankchimero.com/blog/2015/the-webs-grain/). Not really what bears are supposed to do—and they’ll probably never be good at it—but it’s novel and functioning!

However, the task of making something *good*—of arriving at a solution that is obvious—is often met with a kind of ambivalence, like: *"Nice work… I guess? Seems obvious tbh."*

That’s the work of design: to make something so good, it’s obvious. But there’s often little acclaim for the obvious because, well, it’s so obvious (in hindsight).

## The Responsive Web Example

This plays out in many different ways. For example, consider a task like making a website responsive.

In my experience, it’s often quite easy to get people to say, *"Hey that’s cool, it looks like a mobile site now! Good job!"* Getting to that point is often just a matter of sticking a few media queries in your CSS. People are impressed simply because they aren't honing in on the details of *how* it works—just that it works at all.

> *"Cool, the site displays on a mobile phone now! We can move on."*

**But just because it works doesn’t mean it’s good.**

That extra mile to “it works on mobile *and* it’s also a good experience” is a *ton* of work. 
* Is it fast? 
* Is it accessible? 
* Is it intuitive? 
* Does it work across multiple devices? 
* Can it be iterated on quickly? 

So. Many. Questions.

## The Binary vs. The Subjective

* **“Does it work?”** is a binary question.
* **“Is it good?”** is a subjective question whose answer lives at the intersection of multi-disciplinary knowledge and taste, which is to say: it’s infinitely harder to answer.

When we decide to build something, the process generally boils down to two stages:
1. Make it work.
2. Make it good.

To **“make it work,”** all you gotta do is get it running. Consensus on when to applaud and reward the work is simple because it’s either working or it’s not.

To **“make it good”** requires all kinds of nuanced labor. Consensus on when to applaud and reward this work is often impossible to discern because not everyone agrees on what “good” actually looks like.

As the saying goes: *“Make it work” is the first 90% of the work. “Make it good” is the other 90%.*
