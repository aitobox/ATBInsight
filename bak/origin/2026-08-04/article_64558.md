# Giving and Taking Credit in Big Tech Companies

### Summary
Many engineers enter the tech industry with a "school mindset," believing that high-quality work will be automatically recognized and rewarded by their managers. This is a misconception. In large, complex organizations, managers often lack the technical depth to evaluate individual performance accurately. Instead, they rely on informal networks of trusted engineers to gauge value. To succeed, engineers must move beyond simply "doing the work" and learn the strategic art of managing credit and blame—a process that involves both self-promotion and, counterintuitively, the generous sharing of credit with others.

---

### The "School Fantasy"
Engineers often expect their managers to handle "visibility," assuming that if they focus on the code, their performance will be objectively graded. This is an extension of the academic environment, where assignments are clearly defined and rewards are meritocratic. However, big tech companies are not schools. If you wait for your manager to notice your work, you will likely be overlooked. Because software systems are too complex for any one manager to fully understand, they cannot reliably assess your technical output. They are forced to rely on the opinions of others, making visibility a necessary component of your career.

### The Art of Taking Credit
Experienced engineers understand that they must actively manage their own narrative. This involves:
*   **Communicating Impact:** Regularly updating your manager on your progress.
*   **Internal Documentation:** Writing posts that explain technical challenges and solutions.
*   **Building Trust:** Establishing relationships with the management chain so they understand the value you bring.

This is not just "playing politics"; it is a necessary survival skill in an environment where your work is otherwise invisible.

### The Power of Giving Credit
A common mistake is hoarding credit for solo projects. While this might seem like a way to ensure you get all the recognition, it is actually a strategic disadvantage. 
*   **Credit is not zero-sum:** When you share credit, you incentivize others to become advocates for your work. 
*   **Building Allies:** By publicly thanking collaborators and involving others in your projects, you build a network of people who are invested in your success. 
*   **Collective Success:** Projects that involve multiple people are viewed more favorably because they demonstrate teamwork and cooperation, whereas isolated projects can make an engineer appear antisocial or unhelpful.

### The Dynamics of Blame
Blame follows the same social mechanics as credit. In complex systems, failures are rarely the fault of a single person. When something goes wrong, managers look to their networks to assign responsibility. 
*   **The Risk of Isolation:** If you are the only person associated with a project, you become the sole target when things go wrong. 
*   **The Safety of Collaboration:** If you have shared the credit for a project, you have also created a group of stakeholders who are incentivized to defend the project and help troubleshoot, rather than scapegoating you.

### Conclusion
Credit and blame are the primary currencies of the tech industry. To thrive, you must stop waiting for an "expert grader" to notice your efforts. Instead, you must balance self-advocacy with a culture of shared credit. By making your work a team effort, you not only amplify your own reputation but also build a protective network that supports you during both successes and failures.
