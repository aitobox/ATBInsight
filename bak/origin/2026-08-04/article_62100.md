# Summary

In this July 21, 2026 entry of *Pluralistic*, Cory Doctorow explores **"dickovers"**—John Gruber's term for the irritating modal panels, popovers, and cookie-consent curtains that obscure web content. Doctorow examines how these annoyances are weaponized under the guise of GDPR compliance, why big tech companies hypocritically refuse to remember user privacy preferences, and how users can reclaim their digital lives using tools like **Firefox**, **Reader Mode**, **Kill Sticky**, and **Adblock Plus**. Framing ad-blocking as a necessary form of self-defense and "bargaining" against data plunder, Doctorow urges tech-literate readers to help friends and family disenshittify their devices.

# Dealing with dickovers ([Permalink](https://pluralistic.net/2026/07/21/dickovers/#block-element))

One of 2026's better tech-related coinages is "dickover," John Gruber's term for:

> a modal panel, popover, or curtain presented by a website or app, deliberately obscuring its own content to frustrate the user with an unwanted, unnecessary, mandatory interaction; e.g. asking the user to accept “cookies”, subscribe to a newsletter, install the website’s mobile app, agree to terms of service, or anything else that the user couldn’t give two shits about.  
> — [Daring Fireball](https://daringfireball.net/2026/05/what_is_a_dickover)

These are bad everywhere, but especially terrible in the UK and EU, where websites practice malicious compliance with the GDPR. Under the law, websites must secure affirmative consent to process your data—which *should* mean not collecting your data unless necessary, but instead results in endless cookie-consent dialogs.

Your browser can transmit a ["Global Privacy Control signal"](https://support.mozilla.org/en-US/kb/global-privacy-control), but many websites respond by throwing up a GPC dickover forcing you to click through anyway. Web operators falsely claim laws like GDPR or CCPA require this; in reality, every privacy law includes exceptions for "legitimate interests"—such as remembering a user's choice not to be tracked. 

Surveillance giants like Facebook claim that spying on you is a *contractual obligation* mandated by their Terms of Service, yet argue that remembering your desire *not* to be tracked doesn't fall under "legitimate interest."

### How to Fight Back

While stonking fines are the ideal cure, users have powerful tools to banish dickovers. Note that these methods work best on **desktop browsers (specifically Firefox)**, rather than mobile apps or heavily restricted iOS browsers:

1. **Reader Mode:** Built into Firefox, it strips away clutter into clean text. Toggle it via the page-view icon or `ctrl-alt-r`. You can force it globally via `about:config` by toggling `reader.parse-on-load.force-enabled` to `true`.
2. **Kill Sticky:** A bookmarklet that deletes static elements (like annoying headers and overlays) that refuse to move when you scroll. Try [gala8y's fork](https://github.com/gala8y/kill-sticky--forked).
3. **Adblock Plus (ABP):** Use the [ABP browser extension](https://adblockplus.org/) and its "Block Element…" context-menu feature to permanently hide a site's dickovers.
4. **Remove Paywall:** Use [Remove Paywall](https://www.removepaywall.com/) to bypass soft paywalls and nagging registration screens.

We have an obligation to help our non-technical loved ones—our "normies"—seize the means of computation. By configuring their devices to block dickovers and ads, we protect their privacy and permanently improve their internet experience. Ad-blocking isn't theft; it's **self-defense** against a generation of unrestricted data plunder.
