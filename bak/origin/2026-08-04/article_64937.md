# Post-American Compute for a Post-American Internet

Obviously, the non-American world has a digital sovereignty problem—Trump has the means, motive, and opportunity to order his tech companies to shut down any public official, large corporation, or individual who displeases him ([Their Trillions, Our Billions](https://pluralistic.net/2026/06/18/their-trillions-our-billions/#eyes-on-the-prize)). 

But *Americans* face the exact same digital sovereignty risk. America is a lawless place now, where a pliable Supreme Court and supine Congress have affirmed that "it's not a crime if the president does it." The same tech giants who sold out to Trump for tax breaks and protection from antitrust enforcement will happily disconnect any member of the American public, any American company, or any official who falls out of favor.

It's a strange irony that while many of us struggle to "de-Google" our lives, a forcible, sudden de-Googling amounts to a sort of digital death penalty ([NYT: Google Surveillance](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/08/21/technology/google-surveillance-toddler-photo.html)). In a world dominated by tech monopolies and cartels, we seek protection from companies that are "too big to care"—and yet, that very dominance makes them indispensable.

Take "ICE Block," an iOS app warning users if immigration enforcement is nearby, potentially saving them from harassment, detention, or deportation. To protect its relationship with the Trump regime and secure tariff-free access to Chinese labor, Apple declared ICE officers a "protected class" and removed ICE Block from the App Store ([Rogue Capitalism](https://pluralistic.net/2025/10/06/rogue-capitalism/#orphaned-syrian-refugees-need-not-apply)). 

Big Tech is key to Trump's regime. Without Oracle's databases, Microsoft's administrative tools, Amazon's cloud, and Google's location data, ICE would be paralyzed. Because Big Tech is the source of Americans' vulnerability to authoritarian oppression, Americans *cannot* rely on Big Tech to protect them. Yet, after decades of lax antitrust enforcement, Big Tech has forced nearly all rivals out of business.

As an old eastern Canadian joke goes: *"If you wanted to get there, I wouldn't start from here."*

### Enter the Technology Freedom Cooperative

While Canada has its problems, it is not the USA—though it is connected via the internet. Hypothetically, Americans could source their computing infrastructure from suppliers based in Canada that strictly ensure no dependency on U.S. services or "enforcement nexus" ([They're Still Trying to Ban Cryptography](https://pluralistic.net/2023/03/05/theyre-still-trying-to-ban-cryptography/)).

That is *exactly* what human rights nonprofits have done. The **Technology Freedom Cooperative** is a brand-new organization founded by:
* Human Rights Data Analysis Group (San Francisco)
* Kilómetro 0 (Puerto Rico)
* Invisible Institute (Chicago)
* Data Cívica (Mexico)
* Innocence & Justice Louisiana

([TechFreedomCoop Announcement](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/techfreedomcoop-stuart-flack-8eipc/))

These organizations have long histories of collecting and analyzing data to address systemic discrimination, false imprisonment, and war crimes. They concluded they can no longer rely on U.S. tech and servers—especially after Trump and Microsoft colluded to kill the online accounts of the Chief Prosecutor of the International Criminal Court for pursuing a genocide warrant against Netanyahu ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/icc-trump-sanctions-karim-khan-court-a4b4c02751ab84c09718b1b95cbd5db3)).

The Cooperative has federated computing resources across Canada, the U.S., Mexico, Puerto Rico, and Europe. By distributing data and computation across multiple jurisdictions, a U.S. seizure or deletion order cannot halt their work. This federated system replaces Big Tech administrative tools (email hosting, cloud storage, document collaboration) and builds out locally hosted, managed AI infrastructure.

### A Realistic Approach to AI and Human Rights

Groups like HRDAG have decades of experience using advanced statistical techniques to uncover crimes during civil wars and genocides:
* Building the largest human rights database to track deaths in the **Colombian Civil War** ([HRDAG Colombia](https://hrdag.org/colombia/))
* Conducting the first census of killings by **U.S. police officers** ([HRDAG Poli](https://hrdag.org/poli/))
* Partnering with the **Innocence Project New Orleans** to review arrest reports and help innocent people win freedom ([HRDAG IPNO](https://hrdag.org/2025/02/20/ipno/))
* Tracking crimes committed by the Trump regime and documenting genocide in Gaza ([HRDAG Gaza](https://hrdag.org/pressroom/nyt-gaza-toll/))

Patrick Ball, founder of HRDAG, is a prime example of a "centaur"—a skilled practitioner who uses coding assistants to handle massive, vital data-analysis projects ([Pop That Bubble](https://pluralistic.net/2025/12/05/pop-that-bubble/#u-washington)). While the AI bubble is madness and economic ruin ([The AI Will Continue](https://pluralistic.net/2026/05/26/the-ai-will-continue/#until-morale-improves)), AI itself is simply another form of statistical inference. Used wisely, it has valid applications. 

The Coop is running open models based on their own data, on hardware they own and control, with the goal of helping human rights organizations verify that models are accurate, reproducible, and secure. Finally, the Coop provides essential **training**—teaching human rights workers how to administer their own servers, secure communications, and analyze data safely.

At last, we are seeing the first steps toward a post-American internet.
