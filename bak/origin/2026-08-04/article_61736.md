# Mornings in Cupertino Have the Aroma of Napalm Once Again

## Summary
In response to Apple’s recent, aggressive lawsuit against OpenAI over talent poaching and trade secrets, industry commentary suggests that Apple has reverted to its classic, combative playbook. While Tim Cook has historically favored diplomacy, incoming leadership signals and historical parallels—such as Steve Jobs’s ferocious anti-poaching emails to Adobe and Palm—reveal a renewed willingness to wage corporate warfare. Rather than a sign of weakness, this passion-fueled litigation highlights Apple's deep-seated refusal to let competitors dismantle its core industrial design teams without a fight.

---

## ‘It Seems You Have a Different Policy’

Ben Thompson, in a subscriber-only *Stratechery* update, shared a telling email from former Apple executive and Nest founder Tony Fadell regarding Apple’s lawsuit against OpenAI:

> Good article as always… This is Apple’s typical tactic to scare Apple employees — either former or current. I heard this lawsuit was driven by the Apple board.
> 
> Steve threatened to file a lawsuit against Nest for poaching 80-100 Apple employees. He called me, screamed for a while with lots of accusations. Then I said, “Steve, it’s Apple’s job to retain its talent, not mine.” He stopped his rant and then we went on to talk about our families and vacation plans. We kept hiring…

Fadell is correct that it is Apple’s job to retain its talent. Recent reporting from Mark Gurman suggests that John Ternus is keenly aware of this, particularly concerning Apple’s industrial design team, which has been ground zero for the Apple-to-OpenAI recruitment pipeline.

Playing hardball regarding poaching is deeply embedded in Apple’s DNA. Steve Jobs detested losing employees to competitors. In May 2005, he famously emailed Adobe CEO Bruce Chizen:

> **From:** Steve Jobs  
> **To:** Bruce Chizen  
> **Date:** Thursday, May 26, 2005 9:36 AM  
> **Subject:** Recruiting  
> 
> Bruce,  
> 
> Adobe is recruiting from Apple. They have hired one person already and are calling lots more. I have a standing policy with our recruiters that we don’t recruit from Adobe. It seems you have a different policy. One of us must change our policy. Please let me know who.  
> 
> Steve

A more contentious example followed in August 2007, when Jobs sent a blistering email to Palm CEO Ed Colligan over active recruitment fueled by former Apple insiders Jon Rubinstein and Fred Anderson. 

### The Backstory: Key Players
* **Ed Colligan:** A business-first executive who famously underestimated product-driven competitors.
* **Jon Rubinstein:** A legendary hardware engineering executive who oversaw Mac hardware and later the iPod division before departing for Palm via Elevation Partners.
* **Fred Anderson:** Apple’s CFO through the harrowing turnaround years of the late '90s and early 2000s, who later parted on bad terms following a stock options backdating scandal.

While Apple, Google, Adobe, and others ultimately paid a collective $415 million penalty in a 2015 anti-poaching class-action settlement, the underlying philosophy remains: rules are often only followed by those who fear the penalties. 

Steve Jobs *liked* to fight. Tim Cook, by contrast, is a diplomat rather than a general. However, by taking Jobs’s parting advice—*"Don’t ask what I would do. Just do the right thing"*—to heart, Cook has occasionally steered the company differently than Jobs would have. With John Ternus stepping up, the pendulum may be swinging back toward a "what would Steve do?" mentality.

## ‘This Is Not Satisfactory to Apple’

In the early 1990s, Apple infamously filed a "look and feel" lawsuit against Microsoft as Windows gained ground in the corporate market. It was an incoherent, desperate low point that ended in a crushing legal defeat while Apple hovered near bankruptcy. 

When Apple announced its recent lawsuit against OpenAI, initial skepticism questioned whether it carried that same whiff of loser desperation. However, unlike the 1990s Microsoft debacle, this move lacks strategic incoherence. Apple’s message is simple: *"You want to fuck with us? We’ll fuck with us."* When you poke a bear 400 times, you must be prepared for it to react.

If Apple’s claims regarding trade-secret chicanery are unfounded, OpenAI has nothing to fear, and Ternus will look feckless. But if Apple is right, all bets are off. OpenAI cannot easily settle this through a cash payout, because Apple will likely accept nothing short of a complete accounting of the threat to its hardware division. 

Ultimately, this lawsuit is a passion-fueled reaction. A bad corporate look can still be a powerful, inspiring thing. As history shows, when Apple is pushed to the edge, it doesn't fold—it goes to war.
