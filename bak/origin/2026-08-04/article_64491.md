# Put A Tag On It: The Surprising History of Pricing Guns and Barcodes

> **Summary:** Long before digital barcodes and modern electronic shelf labels sparked debates about dynamic pricing and surveillance, retail relied on the humble pricing gun. This article explores how a clever mix of typewriters, combination locks, and self-stick adhesives created a revolutionary device in the mid-20th century—and how state laws artificially kept the pricing gun alive for decades as a shield against the "scary" barcode.

---

## Introduction

Determining how much something costs shouldn’t be difficult. However, in an ecosystem driven by highly personalized data, concerns are rising that prices might not be on stable ground. 

Recently, some states and regulators have begun to scrutinize e-paper price tags out of concern that these tags enable pricing that adapts to demand—or worse, to the individual customer. A major union, the United Food and Commercial Workers International Union (UFCW), even announced plans to launch a campaign against electronic shelf labels. 

But this concern is not new. It more or less played out 50 years ago with a technology most people take for granted today: the barcode, and its predecessor, the pricing gun.

## By the Numbers: 200,000 Pricing Guns

That is the number of pricing guns that **Sears** used during a 1989 promotional campaign that permanently lowered prices across its U.S. stores. The retailer also used 100 million pricing labels—roughly equal to the dollar amount spent on the campaign. The event required closing stores for 42 hours, with a single store marking down approximately 1 million individual items.

## The Pricing Gun: Where Did It Come From?

Searching for a "pricing gun" online is surprisingly difficult due to keyword filters. Yet, the pricing gun remains an iconic retail tool born from the synthesis of entirely unrelated inventions: combination locks, typewriters, and rubber stamps.

### The Mechanics of Innovation
The pricing gun relies on three core functions:
1. **Self-Stick Adhesive:** An innovation by Avery Dennison that made mass labeling possible. Hand-writing and cutting stickers for every grocery store product would be the ultimate definition of tedium.
2. **The Printing Press:** Handheld rubber stamps and self-inking mechanisms removed the need for inkwells.
3. **Combination Locks:** In the 1870s, innovators realized that outward-facing numbers on combination locks could function like a printing press.

This confluence of ideas led to the **Bates Automatic Numbering Machine**, patented around 1901. While originally meant for checking legal documents, its press-and-turn mechanism mirrored how price taggers would eventually operate.

### From Pins to Dials
At the turn of the 20th century, Frederick Kohnle of the Automatic Pin Ticketing Machine Company adapted this process for clothing tags. His work laid the foundation for Monarch Tag and Label (now owned by Avery Dennison).

In the 1950s, Monarch developed the **Dial-A-Pricer**, while inventors like Ed Garvey introduced competing price stampers. Although a 1940s patent by Robert S. Kafka for Midland Equipment Corp. closely resembles a modern pricing gun, it was never commercialized on a large scale. 

Ultimately, companies like Monarch spread the technology widely, giving grocers the power to quickly raise or lower prices by slapping new stickers over old ones.

## How Michigan Became Ground Zero for the Price-Tag Debate

In the late 1970s, the introduction of the barcode threatened to make item-level price tags obsolete. However, consumer protection advocates and labor unions pushed back.

> *"There is an attempt being made to replace clearly marked prices on items with unintelligible lines that can only be deciphered by a computer,"* warned William C. Marshall, president of the Michigan State AFL-CIO, in a 1976 wire story. *"This would make comparison shopping in the supermarkets impossible and could greatest ripoff of consumers imaginable."*

### The Strict Michigan Law
Passed in 1976, Michigan law forced grocery stores and retailers to individually price-tag tens of thousands of items, even if a shelf label was present. Failure to comply resulted in fines of up to $25,000. 

Retailers complained bitterly about the crushing labor costs. Home improvement retailer Builders Square estimated that **one-tenth of its total store labor cost** in the state went solely toward price tagging ($2.4 million). 

Despite heavy lobbying, the law remained on the books for decades, finally being repealed well after most other states had abandoned similar mandates. By that time, the pricing gun was largely a relic.

## Security Risks and the Decline of the Pricing Gun

While pricing guns made manual labeling fast, they also introduced unexpected risks:
* **The Threat of Fraud:** Stolen pricing guns allowed bad actors to forge low-price stickers, prompting retailers to favor centralized barcode systems.
* **Unintended Violence:** Because pricing guns are bulky and somewhat resemble firearms from a distance, they were occasionally involved in tragic confrontations when retail employees mistook them for—or used them to deter—robbers.

Ultimately, the pricing gun’s golden age lasted only about 20 years before barcodes took over the retail landscape entirely.

---

## History Repeating Itself: Electronic Shelf Labels

Today, the backlash against e-paper shelf labels and dynamic pricing mirrors the panic over barcodes 50 years ago. While industry groups insist that digital tags are merely used for daily updates or clearance items rather than aggressive surge pricing, consumer skepticism remains high. 

While the pricing gun may still find niche uses—such as stamping expiration dates on small-batch goods—we have long moved past the era of manually tagging every single item on the shelf.
