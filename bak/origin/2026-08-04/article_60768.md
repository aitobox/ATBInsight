# Microspeak: Double-click and drill down

## Summary
*Microspeak* encompasses not just terms exclusive to Microsoft, but essential corporate jargon required to navigate its culture. This article explores the evolution of investigative corporate buzzwords—tracing the journey from the traditional **"drill down"** to the modern, cringeworthy **"double-click."***

---

## The Rise of "Drill Down"

For most of my career at Microsoft, people wanting to sound important while asking to delve deeper into a topic would use the phrase **"drill down."** 

> *“Bob added a section on accessibility. We may need to drill down into specifics for each of the requirements.”*

> *“We conducted a survey to determine how people use our feature. They broke down into four general categories listed here. Let’s drill down a bit further into each of these categories.”*

Over time, the verb phrase was **nouned** into *drill-down* (representing the result) and used as an adjective (meaning "related to the act of drilling down").

> *“Here is the drill-down.”*
> *“From that number, two drill-down paths exist.”*

### Official Microsoft Style Guide Stance
The term eventually gained official recognition in the [*Microsoft Writing Style Guide*](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/welcome/), featuring [its own dedicated entry](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/d/drill-down-drill-up-drill-through):

> **drill down, drill up, drill through**
> 
> It’s OK to use *drill down*, *drill up*, and *drill through* in content about data and the reports generated from them.
> 
> * **Grammar rules:** Two words as a verb. Hyphenate *drill-down* and *drill-up* as adjectives. Note that *drillthrough* as a noun or an adjective is one word, no hyphen.
> 
> **Examples in Data Analysis:**
> * If you have grouped items in your PivotTable, you can **drill down** on a group name. Then, you can **drill back up** to analyze summary data.
> * Using **drill-down** and **drill-up** actions, you can expand and collapse sections within a report...
> 
> **What to Avoid:**
> Don’t use the terms to mean following a path (such as folders) or giving something further examination.
> * *Incorrect metaphorical usage:* Microsoft MVPs joined an **in-depth discussion** about Azure security management solutions (rather than a "drill down").

Essentially, the style guide’s anti-guidance inadvertently defines the classic Microspeak definition of *drill down*: **"To give something further examination."**

---

## Enter the New Contender: "Double-Click"

While metaphorical and literal "drill downs" remain in wide use, around 2020 or 2021 I encountered a newer, far more jargony equivalent—a trend later covered by [*The Wall Street Journal*](https://www.wsj.com/lifestyle/workplace/double-click-corporate-buzzword-cringe-421e8cce) and amplified by the [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=64965) in 2024.

I am referring to the phrase **"double-click."**

As far as I can tell, *double-click* means roughly the same thing as *drill down*, but with an added layer of corporate cringe. Finding written citations is difficult because people seem too embarrassed to type it into official documents, but it thrives in spoken conversation:

> *“There are currently no resources devoted to designing a tutorial. Once we have resources, we can **double-click** on the journey for how users can find it.”*

> **Person 1:** *“If the user clicks the button, we will take them to a settings page where they can enable their widgets.”*  
> **Person 2:** *“I want to **double-click** on this experience. Why does the user need to explicitly enable their widgets? Is there a path where we can enable them by default?”*

### Final Verdict
I sincerely wish people would stop using *double-click* in this fashion. It bothers me about as much as the nounified [*ask*](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/oldnewthing/20040107-00/?p=41173).
