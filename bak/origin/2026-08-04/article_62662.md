## 📌 Executive Summary

This article explores whether construction costs can be successfully reduced by targeting the basic inputs of the building process—specifically **labor** and **materials**. 

* **The Baking Analogy:** While capturing economies of scale is like shifting from a home kitchen to an industrial bakery, lowering input costs is like changing the recipe to require fewer or cheaper ingredients.
* **Labor Challenges:** Conventional on-site construction prevents builders from easily relocating to low-cost labor regions. Prefabrication opens this door slightly, but heavy transportation costs, capital requirements, and logistical limits (e.g., the 500-mile trucking limit) heavily erode those savings.
* **Material Challenges:** Structural engineers already push the material efficiency frontier close to its absolute limit. While many basic building materials (like concrete, steel, and siding) have manufacturer profit margins high enough to theoretically allow for lower prices, severe regulatory barriers—such as the difficulty of permitting new quarries or cement plants—and industry-wide risk aversion suppress true competition and cost-cutting.

---

## Introduction

In our series on the nature of the construction productivity problem, we previously examined **economies of scale** and found that capturing them in homebuilding is remarkably difficult. Economies of scale depend on the gap between the cost of raw material inputs and the final product. In housing, that ratio is already quite lean—closely matching high-volume manufacturing sectors like the auto industry that have already maximized those advantages.

However, a second route for reducing construction costs remains: **reducing the cost of inputs directly**, either by using cheaper inputs (less expensive materials or labor) or by using fewer of them. 

---

## Finding Cheaper Construction Labor is Hard

Historically, manufacturers reduced labor expenses by relocating operations to regions with cheaper labor. As noted in *[The Origins of Efficiency](https://press.stripe.com/origins-of-efficiency)*:

> *Garment manufacturing, for example, has proven resistant to automation and remains a labor-intensive industry. As a result, garment manufacturing operations tend to continuously relocate to sources of low-cost labor... From Manhattan to Brooklyn and New Jersey, then to New England, then... to the South, and, finally, overseas. Similarly, Nike began importing footwear from Japan in the 1960s... then to Taiwan and Korea, then China, then Vietnam and Indonesia.*

This relocation strategy fails in traditional construction for an obvious reason: **conventional building happens on-site** and cannot be easily moved.

### The Limits of Prefabrication
Off-site or modular construction bypasses this restriction, but introduces new complications, primarily the high cost of transporting prefabricated components. Most manufactured home producers face a strict practical limit of about **500 miles**—the maximum distance a truck can drive in a single day.

Though regional labor cost differentials exist in the US—with a roughly $2\times$ cost gap between the 10th and 90th percentile cities according to the [RSMeans City Cost Index](https://www.rsmeans.com/media/wysiwyg/quarterly_updates/2021-CCI-LocationFactors-V2.pdf)$^1$—modular builders face diminishing returns:
* **Autovol** builds modules in Nampa, Idaho, and ships them hundreds of miles to high-cost markets in Los Angeles and the Bay Area, boasting lower relative costs.
* **Stack Modular** builds in China and transports parts via bulk carriers, but only claims benefits like "cost certainty" and "reduced schedule," rather than radical hard-cost savings.
* **Volumetric Building Companies (VBC)** notes that prefabrication cost savings are typically modest (5–10%) or frequently fail to materialize at all.

Because construction cannot freely relocate, it shares this structural constraint with the agriculture industry—likely explaining why both sectors employ the [largest shares of undocumented labor](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2025/08/21/u-s-unauthorized-immigrant-population-reached-a-record-14-million-in-2023/).

## Lowering Material Costs is Hard

The alternative strategy is to lower material costs by using **fewer materials** or **less expensive materials**.

### Using Fewer Materials
While innovations like concrete 3D printing promise optimized material placement, existing structural design is already exceptionally close to the material efficiency frontier. As noted in a [previous essay on reducing building materials](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-valuable-are-building-methods):

> *Structural elements are designed to be as materially efficient as possible, and have been for many decades. Wood trusses... wood I-joists... Steel W-sections... and light gauge steel framing are standard... Even heavy, cheap materials like concrete are used in materially efficient ways.*

When structural material use isn't minimized, it is usually because further reductions introduce undesirable trade-offs: higher labor costs, structural complexity, increased project risk, or strict **building code restrictions** (such as limitations on low-voltage DC lighting wiring).

### Using Less Expensive Materials
Many key building components are already exceptionally cheap or poor candidates for substitution:
* **Dimensional Lumber:** Makes up roughly 20% of a new house's cost (framing plus labor), yet in dollars-per-cubic-foot terms, it is among the cheapest materials civilization produces. Boise Cascade and Weyerhaeuser reported narrow gross margins (14%–16%), leaving little room for downward pricing pressure.$^2$
* **Drywall:** Though disliked, installing and finishing drywall costs only about $1.50 per square foot. Alternatives like MDF panels, PVC, or Fibo range from $1.50 to $10+ per square foot.

Other materials—like concrete, steel, insulation, and siding—show higher manufacturer gross margins (often ranging from 25% to 45% for companies like Vulcan Materials, Cemex, Owens Corning, and Simpson). However, capturing savings here faces massive bottlenecks:
1. **Permitting Bottlenecks:** Opening a new aggregate quarry or cement plant requires years of bureaucratic approvals (often 7 to 10 years and millions of dollars) and carries high risk of denial.
2. **The Risk Aversion of Design Professionals:** Architects, engineers, and builders rely on established brands and third-party code reports (such as ICC or IAPMO) to avoid catastrophic liability and lawsuits. Specifying unproven, cheaper alternative products introduces unacceptable risk.
3. **Aesthetic Trade-offs:** Cheaper substitute finishes (such as hollow doors over solid ones, or vinyl siding over brick and fiber cement) are often rejected by consumers because they genuinely "feel" cheaper.

---

## Conclusion

The prospects for lowering construction costs by altering the "recipe" of labor and materials are mixed. 

* **Labor costs** are rigid due to the local, on-site nature of construction, and off-site prefabrication solutions are hampered by high transportation and capital overhead.
* **Material costs** have theoretical room for reduction given supplier profit margins, but structural designs are already hyper-efficient, and external barriers—such as severe permitting restrictions, import tariffs, and the rational risk-aversion of design professionals—make substantial cost-cutting extraordinarily difficult in practice.
