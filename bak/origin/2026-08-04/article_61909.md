# Public Transport: Don't Make Me Think!

## Summary
Navigating public transport ticketing across different cities often turns into a frustrating bureaucratic maze. Applying the core systems design principle—*"Don't Make Me Think"*—this article evaluates the ticketing experiences of various global cities over the past year. From frictionless free passes and seamless contactless payments to absurd multi-app requirements and wasted plastic, the author explores what works, what fails, and why contactless daily-capped payment stands out as the ultimate standard for user-friendly transit.

---

## The Cognitive Burden of Ticketing

In the last year, I've been through over a dozen cities and used public transport in all of them. 

It is *wild* just how confusing and complex buying a ticket can be. While some cities obviously take a user-centred approach to ticketing, others appear to take a Kafkaesque delight in a bureaucratic maze of zones, apps, and intricate restrictions.

One of the seminal texts in computer systems design is "[Don't Make Me Think](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don't_Make_Me_Think)". Every time a service forces the user into making a decision, it is placing a cognitive burden on them. Computers' jobs are to make things easier for us fleshy meatbags.

There are some intractable problems with navigating unfamiliar spaces (looking at you Kyoto Station!), and a transport authority can't publish signs and make announcements in every conceivable language. But there's one thing they *can* control: **ticketing**.

Let me take you through some of the public transport ticket experiences I had—and explain how easy or hard they were to use.

## City-by-City Experiences

### Basel, Switzerland: Free
Upon checking in to our hotel, I was given a *free* [Basel Card](https://www.basel.com/en/baselcard). It entitled me to free public transport across the city at zero extra cost. 
* I didn't have to remember to take a physical card; details were emailed as a PDF directly on check-in.
* I didn't have to show the bus driver when entering a tram (inspectors could scan it if present).

This is close to the platonic ideal of "Don't Make Me Think" public transport. No zones, fares, timings, or interactions with people or machines.

### Milano, Italy: Contactless
I tapped my credit card on the reader for any bus, tram, or metro: *Tap, wait a few milliseconds, beep.* 

While fares have minor complexities based on travel frequency (e.g., 90-minute transfers), [the daily price is capped](https://www.atm.it/en/ViaggiaConNoi/Biglietti/Pages/contactless_cards_metro.aspx). Once you take a few journeys, it won't cost you any more. With a fee-free credit card, it was simplicity itself.

### Warsaw, Poland: Paper Ticket Validation
I walked up to a touchscreen kiosk, selected the 🇬🇧 flag, and bought a 3-day paper ticket using contactless. 

I hopped onto a tram and shoved the ticket into a slot to timestamp it—well, in theory. It didn't work the first few times for inscrutable reasons. After that, I was good to go. While the physical size was slightly inconvenient for a wallet, it generally stayed out of my way.

### Tallinn, Estonia: Scan the App
Install an app, choose a ticket type, pay, done. Every time I entered public transport, I opened the app, clicked the ticket, and waved the QR code over a scanner. 

Choosing a ticket type is a *little* thinky, but the transit app worked smoothly on GrapheneOS, and an optional registration step could be skipped for short-term visitors.

### Riga, Latvia: Scan the Bus
Much like Tallinn: install the app, choose a ticket, pay. 

However, despite buying an all-you-can-eat ticket, I still had to register on *every* single bus or tram. That meant hopping on, finding a QR code, opening the app, and trying to scan it while the driver attempted a new land-speed record. Weird and stressful!

### Melbourne, Australia: Annoying
To visit a mate in the suburbs, we needed to *buy* a physical plastic card and load it up with multiples of $5. Our return fare was about $7.50, meaning our actual journey cost roughly double the fare. 

There was no obvious way to get a refund for the remaining credit or the card itself, so we handed it off to a friend for future tourists.

### Paris, France: Ridiculous
The only way to buy a metro ticket is to download an app. Fair enough. But after downloading the app, it tells you to **install *another* app**! 

*A convoluted, messy, and frustrating situation.* Furthermore, trains and metro travel require separate tickets at different prices, forcing you to carefully calculate your route to avoid overpaying. 

The ticket readers use your phone's NFC chip, so there's no technical reason they couldn't just use standard contactless payment cards. To be fair, change is coming… [in 2030](https://france3-regions.franceinfo.fr/paris-ile-de-france/paris/le-paiement-par-carte-bancaire-bientot-deploye-dans-les-metros-bus-et-rer-on-vous-explique-combien-cela-va-couter-3369688.html)!

### Japan
We picked up a physical Suica card (because the digital one only works with iPhones) and topped it up with cash—a bit of a pain initially. 

However, from then on, it was seamless. The exact same card worked on trains, buses, and trams in Tokyo, Osaka, Hiroshima, and **Kyoto**! Compare that to Germany, where every city demands its own special app. (Note: Japan's public transport system now accepts foreign contactless cards, making it even better for travellers).

## Conclusion: What's Best?

In an ideal world, all public transport would be free and plentiful. In our less-than-ideal reality, it seems obvious that public transport should, above all, be **simple**.

* **Tourists** don't want to grapple with zones, split tickets, random charges, and a different app for every city.
* **Regular travellers** don't want another piece of plastic tat in their wallets or to pre-calculate journey combinations.
* **Cities** save money by avoiding the need to build, develop, and support bespoke apps.

**One app is fine; a different app for every city—even within the *same country*—is infuriating.** 

For the casual visitor, **contactless payment with a daily/weekly cap** is the simplest possible charging mechanism. Visitors should never have to think about how, where, when, or why they need to validate tickets.
